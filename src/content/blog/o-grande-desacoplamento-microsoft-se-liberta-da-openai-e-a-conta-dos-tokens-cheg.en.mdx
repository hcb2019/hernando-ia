## The divorce nobody expected (but everyone saw coming)

Some things you know are going to happen, and yet when they do, the impact still catches you off guard. The Microsoft-OpenAI relationship has always been a strange one — like that couple who lives together, splits the bills, but sleeps in separate bedrooms. On Thursday, Mustafa Suleyman, CEO of Microsoft AI, gave the interview that amounts to signing the divorce papers.

> "We were only sort of set free from our contract with OpenAI about six months ago to formally pursue superintelligence," Suleyman told VentureBeat in an exclusive sit-down at Microsoft Build 2026.

Let me translate: for three years, Microsoft's AI strategy was OpenAI. Full stop. The $13 billion invested gave Microsoft early access to the most advanced models on the planet, catapulted Copilot into the enterprise mainstream, and added hundreds of billions of dollars to the company's market cap. It was a marriage of convenience that worked.

But six months ago, something changed in the contract. Microsoft gained formal authorization to train its own frontier models. And on Thursday, it showed its hand: **seven AI models developed entirely in-house**, branded as the MAI family. The flagship, MAI-Thinking-1, is a reasoning model with 35 billion active parameters.

This isn't a side project. This is Microsoft saying: "we can build too."

## Reid Hoffman: the godfather steps aside

As if the timing couldn't be more symbolic, on the same day Microsoft announced Reid Hoffman is leaving the company's board. Hoffman, who joined when Microsoft bought LinkedIn for $26.2 billion in 2016, was the central figure in the Microsoft-OpenAI bridge. He was on the board when Microsoft invested its first billion into OpenAI in 2019. He was an original OpenAI investor and served on its board until 2023.

Now he's going "founder mode" at Manus, his AI-powered drug discovery startup.

The godfather of the Microsoft-OpenAI marriage is walking out the front door just as Microsoft builds its own model lab. If that isn't an inflection point, I don't know what is.

## The bill has arrived — and it's not cheap

While the tech giants rearrange their alliances, the rest of the industry is having a much more down-to-earth reality check: **the token bill**.

TechCrunch published a piece on Friday that should be mandatory reading for every CTO. The numbers are brutal:

- **Uber** burned through its entire 2026 AI coding budget by **April**. That's right: four months to spend what was planned for the whole year.
- **Microsoft** (yes, that Microsoft) revoked its developers' Claude Code licenses months after enabling them.
- A **Priceline** employee said their Cursor contract renewal came back **4 to 5 times more expensive**.

> "The whole conversation shifted from 'tokenmaxxing' and 'go fast' to 'we need guardrails, how do we control this?'" a source told TechCrunch.

The paradox is cruel: per-token prices are falling, but consumption is exploding at an even faster rate. Autonomous agents consume tokens like there's no tomorrow. Companies that gorged themselves in 2025 on all-you-can-eat subscriptions are now scrambling to understand where their money went — and whether any ROI survived the wreckage.

## It's not all doom and gloom: the flip side

It'd be easy to paint a disaster picture, but reality is more nuanced. That same Friday, **Supabase** — the open-source database that became the darling of "vibe-coding" — announced a **$500 million Series F** at a **$10 billion** valuation. The valuation doubled in 8 months.

The growth? Over 600% in the past year, with more than 60% of new databases being launched "by some sort of AI tool," according to CEO Paul Copplestone.

Supabase is the perfect counterpoint to the doom narrative: while some companies are burning money on tokens without knowing why, others are riding the AI adoption wave sustainably. The difference isn't whether you use AI — it's **how** you use it.

## What this means for you, who are neither Microsoft nor Uber

Three takeaways that matter:

**1. The model monopoly is crumbling.** Microsoft building its own frontier models, Anthropic about to IPO, Meta, Google, xAI — the AI model market is becoming a competitive oligopoly, not a monopoly. This is excellent for consumers: prices drop, options multiply.

**2. The "unlimited token" era is over.** If your company isn't measuring per-task cost for AI agents, you're flying blind. What was "innovation" in 2025 becomes "waste" in 2026. Metrics matter.

**3. The game has shifted from "which model?" to "which platform?".** Microsoft's announcement wasn't just about models — it was about the ecosystem: Foundry IQ, Fabric IQ, Web IQ, Microsoft IQ. The battle is no longer about who has the best LLM. It's about who delivers the best integrated system of agents, context, governance, and security.

The honeymoon is over. But a marriage that works is the one that survives the honeymoon — and the AI industry is entering its adult phase.
